Biomolecular emulators · solution scattering · experimental design

Where does BioEmu-1 fail against solution scattering, and what should be measured next?

Natalija Stepurko · October 2026 · natalija-stepurko.com

BioEmu-1 [1] predicts the set of shapes a protein adopts in solution from its sequence. I asked where these predicted ensembles disagree with measurements, and which new measurements would test and improve such a model. As a benchmark, I compared raw BioEmu-1 ensembles with small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles from SASBDB [4], using the ensembles and computed curves that the PeptoneBench authors published [3]. The clearest systematic error ran opposite to my expectation: for disordered proteins the ensembles are too extended. The obstacle for choosing new experiments is prediction: the size of the error on a protein could not be predicted before measuring it. The proposed campaign therefore has two arms, a controlled replication of known discrepancies and a prospective test of the extension bias.

How the benchmark works

SAXS reports the overall size and shape of a protein in solution, averaged over every conformation it adopts, so it tests an ensemble as a whole. BioEmu-1 was trained on predicted structures, molecular-dynamics simulations and measured stabilities [1]. BioEmu conformers have served as a pool for SAXS ensemble selection on a few dozen proteins, where the unweighted pool did not reproduce the data [2], and PeptoneBench ranks generators by their fit after reweighting [3]. This study asks where the raw model fails by protein class and what that implies for the next measurements.

This project re-analyses BioEmu-1 ensembles sampled by the PeptoneBench authors; it does not retrain or re-sample the model. The archive records the BioEmu code version but not the checkpoint.

The eight steps of the pipeline
  1. public input
    439 measured SAXS profiles with sequence, pH and per-residue disorder score: PeptoneDB-SAXS, curated from SASBDB [4, 3].
  2. public input
    100 BioEmu-1 conformers per protein, sampled by the PeptoneBench authors from the sequence alone.
  3. public input
    One scattering curve per conformer, computed by the PeptoneBench authors with Pepsi-SAXS [6] on each profile's q grid.
  4. computed here
    Average the conformer curves with equal weights; fit one scale factor [5] and one constant background; report reduced χ² against the measured errors, and NRMSD, a second score that does not weight by them.
  5. computed here
    Guinier radius of gyration of the data and of the ensemble curve; flags for aggregation (low-angle upturn), over-subtraction (intensity below −3σ) and oligomers (SASBDB measured mass against sequence mass).
  6. computed here
    Maximum-entropy reweighting [7] along a path of decreasing prior strength; the effective sample fraction φ at the first point with χ² ≤ 2 sorts each entry into one of four kinds.
  7. computed here
    The same scoring for 6 comparator models on the shared entries: four ensemble generators and two single-structure predictors as reference baselines.
  8. computed here
    Predict the raw error from features known before measuring; compare acquisition policies on held-out folds; select replication systems by stated rules and write the two-arm campaign.

Raw SAXS fit is worse for disordered proteins, but the size of the gap depends on how error is measured

Each raw ensemble is the uniform average of its conformer curves, scaled to the profile with one factor and one constant background. Of the 399 profiles, 146 meet χ² ≤ 2 (37%, 95% interval 32%–41%). The median raw χ² is 8.9 for disordered and 2.3 for folded proteins (4.3 for partly disordered). Under NRMSD, a deviation of ln I that does not weight points by their reported errors, the class medians are similar: 0.030 folded, 0.034 partly disordered and 0.031 disordered, with overlapping intervals. A poor reduced χ² can reflect both the disagreement with the curve and the precision assigned to the measurement. The size error is examined separately below.

Disordered ensembles are systematically too extended

The radius of gyration, Rg, is the root-mean-square distance of a protein's mass from its centre, a single number for overall size; SAXS measures it from the lowest-angle part of the curve. For disordered proteins the median ensemble size is 21% above experiment from the scattering curves and 35% above from the conformer coordinates.

E4 expected the usual failure of models trained on folded data, over-compaction of disordered chains. The data show the opposite: the median ratio is 1.21 (95% interval 1.12–1.28, two-sided Wilcoxon p < 0.001, n = 41), and 76% of disordered entries lie above 1; folded proteins sit at 0.98. The size discrepancy is also present in coordinate-derived radii: the Cα radius of the sampled conformers is 1.35 times the measured value (1.20–1.47). Cα, full-atom and SAXS-derived radii are different observables, however: for folded proteins the Cα ratio is 0.91 where the curve gives 0.95, an offset because the curve includes side chains and a hydration layer that the Cα radius leaves out. The 35% is therefore not to be read as an exact physical excess; it shows that the extension is present in the conformers before any curve is computed.

Can the disagreement be corrected by changing conformer populations?

Reweighting keeps every conformer fixed and adjusts only how often each one occurs, until the averaged curve matches the profile. Maximum-entropy reweighting [7] does this while keeping the weights as close to uniform as the data allow; relaxing that constraint step by step traces a path from the raw ensemble towards a full fit. The effective sample fraction φ measures how much of the ensemble still carries weight: 1 for uniform weights, near 0 when a few conformers carry it all. The four kinds are read where the path first reaches χ² ≤ 2: raw fit (already there), modestly reweightable (reached with φ ≥ 0.5), strongly reweightable (reached only with φ < 0.5, which requires substantial population changes) and target fit not reached (by the tested reweighting procedure).

Reweighting brings 156 more profiles to χ² ≤ 2, 91 of them only with φ < 0.5; for 97 the target is not reached.

Two cautions. Reweightability is not information gain: SAXS is low-dimensional, and distinct ensembles can give the same curve, so a reweighted fit does not identify the true populations. Not reaching the target does not establish that compatible conformations are absent from BioEmu's distribution; it describes this finite ensemble of 100 conformers, this forward model and this procedure.

Can the error map guide experiments on proteins that have not been measured?

An error map needs the measurement. Choosing a new experiment needs the error predicted before the measurement exists. I fitted predictors of the raw error (log₁₀ χ²) from quantities known before a profile exists: chain length, disorder score and class, sequence composition, the Rg expected from length by scaling laws [9], and the model's own ensemble Rg and conformer spread. Evaluation is 5-fold cross-validation grouped by sequence cluster, repeated five times. I then asked which entries each acquisition policy would pick from held-out folds and scored the picks by the high-discrepancy target yield: the share that are strongly reweightable or whose target fit is not reached. The yield is a proxy for where the model is wrong, not a measure of training value.

The best model explains 5% of held-out variation in the raw error (R² = 0.05; class means alone 0.04), and the tree classifier of the high-discrepancy kinds reaches an AUC of 0.60. Random picks of ten give a yield of 0.48, with a spread of ±0.15 for any single pick. Ranking by predicted error gives 0.58 ± 0.07, +0.10 over random, within about one sd of a single random pick. The acquisition policy fed predictions, the campaign's priority rule with predicted inputs, gives 0.52 ± 0.03. The oracle ranking, which uses the measured SAXS, reaches 1.00: the rule separates the kinds when it sees the measurement, and the missing piece is a predictor of them. The map therefore supports controlled replication of known failures; it does not support a new-protein campaign driven by predicted error.

The priority score is not a basis for recommending measurements. The design proposed ranking systems by log₁₀ raw χ² × a reweighting weight × a tractability weight, with weights set by judgement. Fed the quantities known before a measurement, it picks high-discrepancy profiles at 0.52 against 0.48 for random choice, a difference inside the spread of a single random pick. I report the score and its test, and the campaign below does not rest on it. Arm 1 re-measures deposited systems, so it can use their measured discrepancy directly: it lists the systems that meet stated eligibility rules and have the largest observed raw χ², which are the same twelve the score ranks first; what is dropped is the judgement-set weighting. Arm 2 splits test proteins from controls by a quantity computed from the model and the sequence alone, within the archive's disordered entries whose deposited profiles pass the quality checks.

What should be measured next?

The error map identifies where BioEmu-1 disagrees with deposited data; it cannot yet say where it will disagree on a new protein. The campaign has two arms that follow from that. The full proposal gives constructs, assay, scripted quality criteria, success criteria, contingencies and work packages for an external provider.

Arm 1 · replication: are known discrepancies reproducible under controlled conditions?

High-error, reweightable monomers from the archive are re-measured by SEC-SAXS in one standard buffer and temperature, with tags removed and a concentration series. Standardised measurement reduces condition heterogeneity and tests whether the discrepancy is reproducible. One common buffer and temperature can itself shift some ensembles, so each new profile is compared with the deposited one before it is compared with the model. Arm 2 · hypothesis test: are BioEmu-1's disordered ensembles systematically too extended?

The hypothesis came from this archive, so the arm is a prospective replication test with pre-specified model-based selection and new SAXS measurements. Test proteins are disordered proteins whose BioEmu-1 ensemble Rg exceeds the scaling-law Rg for disordered chains (1.927 N0.598 Å [9]) by more than 10%, a quantity computed from the model and the sequence alone, drawn from the archive's disordered entries whose deposited profiles pass the quality checks, spanning chain length and net charge (19 of 41 disordered entries qualify). The scaling-law-matched controls are disordered proteins of the same length bins whose ensemble Rg is within 10% of the scaling law (13 qualify). In the archive their ratio of ensemble Rg to measured Rg is 1.19 at the median, against 1.36 for the test group, so the controls are not known to agree with experiment. Pre-specified outcome: the median Rgexp/Rgmodel across the test arm from Guinier fits of the new profiles. The bias is refuted if that median is at least 0.95; it is confirmed if it is at most 0.90 in the test arm and at least 0.95 in the controls.

Both arms use size-exclusion-coupled SAXS at three concentrations with a protein standard in every session, and six scripted quality criteria (Guinier linearity, no low-angle upturn, no radiation damage across frames, molecular weight from I(0) within 20% of the sequence mass, no negative intensities, a repeated deposited entry reproduced at χ² < 2). Every batch of twelve constructs re-runs this pipeline, so the error map is updated before the next batch is chosen.

How does BioEmu-1 compare with other ensemble generators?

The archive carries computed curves from other models for the same entries, scored here identically and without reweighting: the ensemble generators Boltz-2, IDPFold, idpSAM, PepTron, and the single-structure predictors AlphaFold2 and ESMFold as reference baselines. On the shared entries BioEmu-1 has the lowest median raw χ² of the seven, and the ordering is kept under NRMSD. PepTron comes closest: it beats BioEmu-1 on 40% of the 399 shared entries (95% interval 36%–45%). BioEmu-1 is the only tested model with a disordered median Rg ratio above 1.1; PepTron sits at 1.01.

Limits

The ensembles are those in the PeptoneBench archive, which records the BioEmu code version but not the checkpoint; re-sampling with the current v1.2 checkpoint is the first follow-up. SASBDB profiles differ in buffer, temperature and construct, none of which the model sees, so part of the raw error is condition mismatch. The forward model is treated as exact; its hydration-shell parameters bias Rg by a few per cent, which is why modestly reweightable cases are read with care. Disorder classes come from a sequence-based predictor. The model comparison is restricted to the six comparators on the shared entries. The prediction and selection tests are retrospective and use 399 entries; a predictor that fails at this size could succeed with more data or with richer features of the model's own ensemble. Profiles with a low-angle upturn above 10%, an intensity more than three standard errors below zero, no valid Guinier region, or a measured mass above 1.6 times the sequence mass are kept out of every statistic (1, 12, 7 and 18 profiles).

Reproducibility

The pipeline runs in named stages (download, score, coordinate radii, analysis, selection test, prediction, envelopes, report) from a locked environment, with unit tests on synthetic data. Derived results are tracked in the repository, and the page build reads every number from them and stops if a sentence that states a direction or a comparison no longer matches. Key code: coordinate radii, acquisition policies, error prediction. The ensembles and computed curves are those published by the PeptoneBench authors [3] (Zenodo 17306061, CC-BY 4.0).
